Maintenance requests in owners' associations

A leak in the corridor, a stuck lift, a dark stairwell. Daily requests are what residents see most of the association's work, and how they are handled shapes how residents judge the whole management.

iDX team · 16 November 2026

1. Common parts or inside the unit?

First, establish where the problem is, because that decides who pays:

  • Common parts (entrances, lifts, corridors, roof, gardens, gates): costs are shared by all owners in proportion to their unit's area, under the Real Estate Units Ownership, Subdivision and Management Law (Arabic), and paid from the association's budget.
  • Inside the unit (the flat's plumbing, AC, doors): the owner's private property, so the owner pays, not the association's fees. Each association's bylaws set the details.

Being clear on this from the start prevents most disputes between owners and management.

2. The right path for a request

  1. Intake: a reference number, problem type, location, a photo, and the reporter's name and unit.
  2. Duplicate check: if the problem is already reported, add the resident as a follower instead of opening a new request.
  3. Routing: maintenance and cleaning to the supervisor, security reports straight to the manager, emergencies to everyone at once.
  4. Inspection: the supervisor finds the cause and checks whether a maintenance contract or developer warranty covers it.
  5. Cost and approval: for common parts, the manager approves spending from the right budget line. Inside a unit, a price is proposed, the manager approves it, then the resident agrees and pays before work starts.
  6. Work and closure: an after photo, a notice to the reporter and everyone following, and a rating request.

3. Rules that protect the management

  • No rejection without approval: if the supervisor wants to reject a request, the manager approves it with the reason, so a valid request is never closed without their knowledge.
  • No in-unit work before payment: protects the association and avoids later disputes over the amount.
  • Transparency with residents: a weekly summary of common-area fixes builds trust and cuts repeat complaints.
  • A record of everything: who reported, who inspected, what it cost and when it closed. You need this when presenting the budget to owners.

Examples of who pays

  • Stairwell lights out: common part, paid from general maintenance.
  • Lift stopped: common part, usually covered by the annual lift contract.
  • Leak under the kitchen sink: inside the unit, the owner pays by separate invoice.
  • Ceiling leak from the unit above: inspection decides; if it's that unit's plumbing its owner may pay, if it's a common part the association pays.

Frequently asked questions

Who pays for the AC inside a flat?

The owner, because it is inside their unit and not a common part.

Can the supervisor reject a request?

Best practice is that no request closes without the manager knowing: the supervisor proposes rejection with a reason, and the manager approves or returns it.

What's the difference between a request and a complaint?

A request is a fault that needs fixing; a complaint is about cleaning, parking or neighbours' behaviour. Each has its own path and owner.

Note

This article is general information, not legal advice. In December 2025, REGA's board approved updating the owners' association law, and the legal process is ongoing, so some details may change. Always check the official texts, REGA and your association's bylaws.
